# Multi-Source Correlation Methodology

## Purpose Statement

Most sophisticated OSINT conclusions are not built on a single decisive piece of evidence — they are built by correlating multiple, individually inconclusive signals into a conclusion that is stronger than any single piece of evidence could support alone. This guide explains how to do this rigorously, and the common analytical traps that undermine it.

---

## 1. The Core Idea

A single weak signal (say, a C3-rated source per `osint-templates/operational-planning/source-verification-framework.md`) proves little on its own. But several genuinely independent weak signals, all pointing toward the same conclusion, can justify meaningfully more confidence than any one of them individually — provided the independence requirement is actually met.

### Worked Example

Suppose you are investigating whether two seemingly unrelated companies share common ownership:

| Signal | Source | Individually |
|---|---|---|
| Both companies share a registered agent | Public registry filing | Weak — shared registered agents are common and often coincidental |
| Both companies' websites use the same Google Analytics tracking ID | Page source inspection | Weak — could reflect a shared web developer rather than shared ownership |
| Both companies' domains were registered on the same day, one minute apart | WHOIS historical data | Weak — could be coincidental timing |
| An officer of one company is listed as an authorized signer on the other's bank filing in a public court exhibit | Court record | Moderate on its own |

No single signal above is close to conclusive. But taken together — a shared registered agent, a shared analytics identifier, near-simultaneous domain registration, and a documented signing relationship — the combination is considerably more persuasive than any one item, because it is increasingly implausible that four independent coincidences would all point the same direction by chance.

---

## 2. The Independence Test

This is the step analysts most often get wrong. Before treating multiple signals as corroborating each other, ask: **do these signals actually come from independent underlying processes, or do they trace back to the same root cause?**

### Genuinely Independent (Good Correlation)

- A registry filing (legal/administrative process) + a technical artifact (analytics ID, set by a developer) + a financial record (court exhibit) are generated by different processes, by different actors, for different reasons. Agreement between them is meaningful.

### Not Independent (False Correlation — A Common Trap)

- Ten news articles that all say the same thing, but nine of them are simply republishing or paraphrasing the first article's original reporting. This is **one source**, repeated, not ten corroborating sources. Always trace a claim back to its original source before counting how many "sources" support it.
- Multiple data broker aggregator sites (see `osint-tools/people-investigation/background-checking/README.md`) that all show the same address for a person — but all of them source their data from the same underlying public record. This is one underlying fact appearing in multiple places, not independent confirmation.

**Rule of thumb:** before adding a signal to your correlation, ask what its underlying data-generating process was. If two signals share a data-generating process, they count as one signal for correlation purposes, not two.

---

## 3. Alternative Explanation Testing (Analysis of Competing Hypotheses)

For any correlated conclusion, explicitly write out at least one plausible alternative explanation and assess it against the same evidence, consistent with the Analysis of Competing Hypotheses technique referenced in `osint-templates/ai-assisted-templates/predictive-intelligence.md`.

**Continuing the worked example above:** an alternative explanation for the four signals might be "both companies independently hired the same corporate services provider, who set up their registration, website, and analytics using the provider's own standard templates and timing." This alternative would predict a shared registered agent and possibly similar registration timing, but would not easily explain a documented signing authority relationship between named officers. Working through this comparison, rather than only listing supporting evidence, is what separates a rigorous correlation from a confirmation-biased one.

---

## 4. Confidence Calibration

Translate your correlated finding into a calibrated confidence statement rather than an unqualified assertion:

| Situation | Appropriate Confidence Language |
|---|---|
| Multiple genuinely independent signals converge, and the leading alternative explanation is significantly less well supported | High confidence |
| Multiple signals converge, but at least one plausible alternative explanation remains reasonably competitive | Medium confidence |
| Signals point in a consistent direction but rely heavily on lower-reliability sources, or independence is only partial | Low confidence, clearly flagged as such |

See `osint-templates/ai-assisted-templates/predictive-intelligence.md` Section 5 for the same confidence-level framework applied to forward-looking judgments.

---

## 5. Common Pitfalls

- **Counting non-independent sources as independent** (see Section 2). This is the single most common error in correlation-based conclusions.
- **Anchoring on the first hypothesis** and only searching for evidence that supports it, rather than genuinely testing alternatives (confirmation bias).
- **Treating a large number of weak signals as equivalent to one strong signal**, regardless of what those signals actually are. Ten extremely weak, non-independent signals do not equal one moderately reliable signal; quality and independence matter more than raw count.
- **Failing to document the correlation logic**, leaving a report reader unable to assess whether the conclusion is well-supported or simply asserted.

---

## 6. Applying This in a Report

When presenting a correlated conclusion in any of the templates in `osint-templates/`, make the correlation logic visible rather than only stating the conclusion:

> "This assessment is based on four independent signals: [list]. Each individually would be inconclusive; taken together, they converge on [conclusion]. The main alternative explanation considered — [alternative] — is less well supported because [reasoning]. Overall confidence: [level]."

This format lets a report's reader (or a future analyst revisiting the case) evaluate your reasoning, not just your conclusion.
